Decision 01 • Priority Setting

ICU • The high-acuity zone

Mr. Carter enters the ICU.

👀 Assess

Frequent focused cardiovascular and respiratory assessments; monitor pain, perfusion, mental status and vital signs.

📈 Monitor

Continuous cardiac rhythm monitoring and close observation for dysrhythmias, ischemia and hemodynamic instability.

💊 Intervene

Administer and monitor ordered therapies, assess response, watch for adverse effects and communicate changes rapidly.

ICU mindset: “What could kill this patient in the next few minutes—and how will I recognize it early?”

Key point: The diagnosis has not changed. The intensity and immediacy of nursing surveillance have.

Important: exact monitoring frequency and interventions depend on the patient, orders, facility policy, and level of acuity.

ICU vs. Step-Down

Same disease. Different nursing priorities.

🔴 ICU

  • High-frequency assessment
  • Continuous/intensive monitoring
  • Rapid recognition of deterioration
  • Hemodynamic stability
  • Complex therapies and immediate response
  • Preventing life-threatening complications

🔵 Step-Down

  • Ongoing cardiac/clinical monitoring
  • Less intensive than ICU
  • Trend recovery and detect changes
  • Medication transition/management
  • Mobility and activity progression
  • Education + discharge preparation

The Takeaway

What changes when the floor changes?

01 • Acuity

The patient’s condition determines how intensive nursing surveillance must be.

02 • Priorities

ICU care emphasizes immediate threats; step-down increasingly emphasizes stabilization and recovery.

03 • Continuity

The core nursing process—assess, plan, intervene, evaluate, communicate—continues in both settings.

Big idea: A patient does not stop being a cardiac patient because they changed rooms. What changes is the level of nursing care required to keep them safe.
Practice Implications

So what does this mean for us?

  • Recognize deterioration early: trends matter, not just single numbers.
  • Match care to acuity: the setting should support the patient’s current level of risk.
  • Communicate clearly: transitions are high-risk moments for missed information.
  • Educate before discharge: use teach-back rather than simply asking, “Do you understand?”
  • Never work from assumptions: follow current orders, facility policy, scope of practice and the patient’s actual assessment.
Clinical mindset: “What is happening? What changed? What does my patient need now? What do I need to communicate?”
